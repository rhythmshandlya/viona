# Cutting and Pacing Skill

## Purpose
Guide precise cutting decisions and pacing strategy for video editing. This skill covers Dmytryk's rules, cut patterns, retention rhythm, content-specific pacing, and when to hold versus cut.

---

## 1. Dmytryk's Seven Rules of Cutting

Edward Dmytryk's rules remain the foundation of film editing. Apply them to every cut:

### Rule 1: Never make a cut without a positive reason
Every cut must serve one of these purposes:
- **Information**: Reveal new visual information the viewer needs
- **Emphasis**: Draw attention to a specific element
- **Pacing**: Maintain or change the rhythm
- **Emotion**: Shift the viewer's emotional state
- **Continuity**: Move the story forward in time or space

If a cut serves none of these, don't make it. A held shot is often more powerful than an unmotivated cut.

### Rule 2: When undecided about the exact frame to cut on, cut long rather than short
- It's easier to trim frames from a long cut than to extend a short one
- In practice: when placing a cut point, err toward 3-5 frames later than your instinct says
- Exception: In high-energy sequences, cut slightly early to maintain momentum

### Rule 3: Whenever possible, cut "in movement"
- Cutting during motion (a gesture, a head turn, a walk) hides the edit
- The eye tracks the motion across the cut and doesn't notice the splice
- In video editing: cut during visual transitions, not during static holds
- For speaker edits: cut during hand gestures or head movements, not during still delivery

### Rule 4: The "fresh" is preferable to the "stale"
- The first few frames of a new shot have the most impact ("fresh")
- The last few frames of an outgoing shot have the least impact ("stale")
- In practice: enter shots slightly early (show the beginning of the action)
- Don't let shots linger after their purpose is fulfilled

### Rule 5: All scenes should begin and end with continuing action
- Don't start a scene from a dead stop or end on a freeze
- Even speaker-only sections should have subtle motion (slight zoom, breathing room)
- For animations: start with elements already in motion, end with elements transitioning out
- This creates the feeling of a living, continuous world

### Rule 6: Cut for proper values rather than for "proper" matches
- If forced to choose between continuity (matching action) and emotional impact, choose impact
- A jump cut that lands on a powerful moment beats a smooth cut to a weak moment
- This is why we sometimes break continuity rules — emotion trumps technique

### Rule 7: Substance first, then form
- Get the content right before worrying about polish
- A rough cut with great timing beats a polished cut with bad pacing
- First pass: nail the section order and content selection
- Second pass: refine cut points and transitions

## 2. Cut Types and Patterns

### Hard Cut (Straight Cut)
The most common and powerful cut. One shot ends, the next begins immediately.

**When to use:**
- Between sections with different treatments (speaker → animation)
- For pace and energy (rapid-fire cuts = high energy)
- When the content provides a natural bridge (audio continuity across visual change)
- Default choice — use hard cuts unless you have a specific reason for something else

**Execution:**
- Cut on the beat (align with speech rhythm or music)
- Cut on action (during movement or gesture)
- Cut on dialogue (between sentences, not mid-word)

### Jump Cut
Two shots of the same subject with a noticeable time jump. Intentionally jarring.

**When to use:**
- Removing filler/dead air from a speaker (the visible jump shows time was removed)
- Creating urgency or energy (rapid jump cuts = high pace)
- Vlog-style content where authenticity > polish
- Montage sequences (showing progression through time)

**When to avoid:**
- Formal/professional content (looks unpolished)
- When the viewer needs to absorb information (jarring breaks concentration)
- More than 3 consecutive jump cuts (becomes disorienting)

**Mitigation:**
If a jump cut is necessary but undesirable:
- Insert a B-roll cutaway between the two positions (hides the jump)
- Add a zoom punch (5-10% zoom change) to disguise the jump as intentional
- Use a brief text-overlay to bridge the visual discontinuity

### L-Cut (Split Edit — Audio Leads)
Audio from the next shot begins before the visual cuts.

**When to use:**
- Transitioning from speaker to B-roll/animation (speaker's voice continues over new visual)
- Building anticipation (hear about something before seeing it)
- Smoothing transitions between topics
- Interview edits (hear the answer start while still seeing the questioner)

**Timing:**
- Audio leads by 10-20 frames (0.3-0.7s at 30fps)
- The overlap should feel natural, not forced
- Works best when the audio content describes or introduces the incoming visual

### J-Cut (Split Edit — Visual Leads)
Visual from the next shot appears before the audio changes.

**When to use:**
- Showing something before the speaker talks about it (visual hook)
- Returning to the speaker after B-roll (see their face before hearing them)
- Creating mystery (what is this? — then the speaker explains)

**Timing:**
- Visual leads by 8-15 frames (0.25-0.5s at 30fps)
- Shorter lead than L-cuts (visuals process faster than audio)
- The gap between visual change and audio change should feel like a breath, not a gap

### Match Cut
Two shots connected by visual similarity (shape, color, movement, composition).

**When to use:**
- Transitions between major sections (conceptual bridge)
- Connecting abstract animation to concrete footage
- Creating visual metaphors (spinning wheel → loading spinner)
- Time transitions (day → night using similar compositions)

**Planning:**
Match cuts require intentional visual design:
- Plan the outgoing shot's final frame to mirror the incoming shot's first frame
- Shared element: same position, similar shape, matching color, parallel motion
- The match doesn't need to be perfect — approximate matches read as intentional

## 3. Retention Rhythm

### The 15-25 Second Rule

Viewer attention needs regular visual refreshment. Every 15-25 seconds, provide a significant visual change.

**Visual change hierarchy** (from most to least impactful):
1. Treatment change (speaker → animation): Maximum refresh
2. Camera angle/framing change: Strong refresh
3. New graphic element (text-overlay appears): Moderate refresh
4. Color or lighting shift: Subtle refresh
5. Zoom change (punch in/out): Minimal refresh

**Minimum refresh by position:**
- First 30 seconds: Change every 5-8 seconds (hook the viewer)
- 30s - 2 minutes: Change every 10-15 seconds (establish rhythm)
- 2 minutes onwards: Change every 15-25 seconds (settled rhythm)
- Last 30 seconds: Change every 8-12 seconds (exit energy)

Map expected viewer attention against the edit plan:

```

     0s   30s   1m   1:30  2m   2:30  3m   3:30
```

Target: Keep attention above 60% throughout. If a section dips below, add visual complexity.

**Attention Boosters** (when a section is underperforming):
- Add a text-overlay with a compelling stat or quote
- Insert a 3-5s B-roll cutaway
- Add a zoom punch (sudden 10% zoom in/out)
- Change background color or lighting
- Add a brief animation overlay (particle effect, icon)

**Attention Drains** (what causes dips):
- Speaker-only > 15s without any visual change
- Repetitive content (same point made differently)
- Low-energy delivery with no visual compensation
- Complex explanation without visual aid
- Third or fourth consecutive section with the same treatment

### Rhythm Patterns

**Staccato Rhythm** (high energy, listicle/tutorial):
```
Short-Short-Medium-Short-Short-Medium-Short-Long
5s - 5s - 12s - 5s - 5s - 12s - 5s - 20s
```
Use for: List items, rapid examples, high-energy content

**Breathing Rhythm** (balanced, most content):
```
Medium-Short-Long-Short-Medium-Short-Long
12s - 5s - 20s - 8s - 15s - 5s - 20s
```
Use for: Standard presentations, tutorials, explanations

**Cinematic Rhythm** (slow, dramatic):
```
Long-Medium-Long-Short-Long-Medium-Long
20s - 12s - 25s - 5s - 20s - 15s - 25s
```
Use for: Documentary-style, emotional content, keynotes

## 4. When to Hold vs. Cut

### Hold the Shot When:

**The speaker is delivering emotional content:**
- Personal story mid-telling (cutting away breaks the intimacy)
- Genuine laughter or reaction (authentic moments need time)
- Building to a punchline (don't cut before the payoff)
- Showing vulnerability (the face tells the story)

**The visual needs time to register:**
- Complex diagram or data visualization (give 3-5s to read)
- Screenshot with important text (2-3s minimum for reading)
- Beautiful animation (don't rush past your own work)
- Establishing shot (let the viewer orient, 2-3s)

**Cutting would be jarring:**
- Mid-sentence (unless intentional jump cut)
- During a natural pause that's part of emphasis
- When the speaker is gesturing and the gesture carries meaning
- In the middle of an animation sequence

### Cut the Shot When:

**The speaker's point is made:**
- The key sentence is delivered, everything after is restatement
- The example is clear, additional elaboration is redundant
- The transition phrase is complete ("so, moving on" — cut immediately after)

**Energy is dropping:**
- Speaker hesitates or searches for words
- Pace slows without dramatic purpose
- Content becomes repetitive
- Filler word density increases

**A visual reference demands it:**
- Speaker mentions a website, product, or person → cut to screenshot
- Speaker describes a process → cut to animation/diagram
- Speaker cites data → cut to visualization

**Rhythm demands it:**
- You're at the 15-25s mark without a visual change
- The previous section was long — this one should be short for contrast
- You're approaching a climactic moment and need to build pace

## 5. Pacing by Content Type

### Tutorial Pacing

```
Step Introduction:     3-5s   (text-overlay: step number + title)
Step Demonstration:    10-20s (screenshot or animation)
Step Explanation:      5-10s  (speaker-only or speaker + overlay)
Transition:            2-3s   (brief, purposeful)
```

**Tutorial-specific rules:**
- Every step gets a visual number (text-overlay: "Step 1", "Step 2")
- Demonstration sections show the actual thing being discussed
- Don't let explanation sections exceed the demonstration (show > tell)
- Progress indicator is valuable (subtle "3 of 7" counter)
- Recap every 3-4 steps with a brief visual summary

### Podcast Pacing

```
Key Point:            10-15s  (text-overlay or animation for the takeaway)
Discussion:           15-25s  (speaker-only with subtle visual accents)
Tangent/Humor:        5-10s   (speaker-only, fast cuts if boring)
Story/Anecdote:       20-40s  (stock-video + speaker, let it breathe)
```

**Podcast-specific rules:**
- Accept longer speaker-only sections (it's a conversation)
- Cut between speakers on natural dialogue rhythm
- Add visual accents (text-overlays, icons) to maintain engagement during long discussions
- Trim tangents ruthlessly — podcast editing is mostly subtraction
- B-roll cutaways every 30-45s to refresh visuals

### Presentation Pacing

```
Slide Content:        15-25s  (screenshot of slide or recreation as animation)
Speaker Transition:   5-8s    (speaker-only between slides)
Data/Chart:           20-30s  (animation with annotation)
Q&A:                  10-15s  (question as text, answer as speaker)
```

**Presentation-specific rules:**
- Never show a real slide for > 10s — recreate as animated elements
- Speaker picture-in-picture during slide content
- Data slides get animation treatment (charts build, numbers count up)
- Transition slides can be cut entirely (just go to next content)

### Interview Pacing

```
Question:             3-5s    (text-overlay or interviewer shot)
Answer Start:         5-10s   (interviewee, let them settle)
Answer Core:          10-20s  (interviewee with visual accents)
Answer Wrap:          3-5s    (natural ending, set up next question)
```

**Interview-specific rules:**
- Show the interviewer asking the question (establishes the dynamic)
- During answers, cut to relevant visuals when the interviewee references things
- Use lower thirds for each speaker's first appearance
- Don't cut during pauses in answers — the interviewee may be gathering thoughts
- Trim "great question" and "that's a really good point" responses

## 6. Cut Point Selection

### Finding the Exact Frame

When you know WHERE to cut, finding the exact frame:

**For speech cuts:**
1. Find the last word of the outgoing content
2. Move 2-3 frames past the end of the last word (let the sound complete)
3. If the speaker inhales before the next sentence, cut during the inhale
4. If there's a pause, cut 3-5 frames into the pause (not at the very start)

**For animation cuts:**
1. Cut at the end of an animation cycle (not mid-tween)
2. If cutting to a new animation, overlap the exit of old and entrance of new by 2-3 frames
3. Never cut during a spring bounce (wait for the settle)

**For music-driven cuts:**
1. Cut on the beat (downbeat is strongest)
2. Cut on the transient (the attack of a drum hit or chord change)
3. If no obvious beat, cut on the breath between musical phrases
4. Slightly before the beat (1-2 frames) feels more energetic than on the beat

### Audio-First Editing

Always make cutting decisions based on audio first, then adjust visuals:

```
1. Listen to the raw audio without watching
2. Mark cut points based on speech rhythm and content
3. Now look at the video at those cut points
4. Adjust by +/- 3 frames for better visual matches
5. Never move a cut point more than 5 frames from the audio-optimal position
```

## 7. Pacing Diagnostics

### Signs of Good Pacing
- Viewer engagement graph stays above 60% (if analytics available)
- No section feels like it drags or rushes
- Visual changes feel motivated, not arbitrary
- The video feels shorter than its actual runtime
- Each section transitions smoothly to the next

### Signs of Bad Pacing

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Video feels slow | Sections too long, low visual variety | Cut 20% from longest sections, add visual accents |
| Video feels rushed | Sections too short, no breathing room | Extend key sections by 3-5s, add breather sections |
| Video feels repetitive | Same treatment/rhythm repeating | Vary treatment types, alternate section lengths |
| Video feels disconnected | Abrupt transitions, no flow | Add L-cuts/J-cuts, use match cuts for major transitions |
| Video feels monotonous | Flat energy curve | Add intensity peaks (animation, text-overlay) every 45-60s |
| Specific section drags | One section disproportionately long | Split into 2 sub-sections or trim by 30-50% |

### Pacing Audit Checklist

Before finalizing an edit:
- [ ] No section exceeds 30s without a visual change
- [ ] The first 10 seconds have at least 2 visual changes
- [ ] Section lengths vary (standard deviation > 3s)
- [ ] Speaker-only sections don't exceed 15s
- [ ] Treatment types don't repeat more than twice consecutively
- [ ] Energy curve has at least 2 peaks and 2 valleys
- [ ] Total trim percentage is 5-15% of original (enough cleanup without gutting)
- [ ] The video end has higher energy than the middle section
